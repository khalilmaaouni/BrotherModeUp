BrotherME for Claude Code

A careful project partner, explained simply

You describe what you want in plain words. BrotherME agrees a plan with you, does the work through the right helpers, and only calls something done when a real check has passed after the last change.

What it actually is

Claude Code out of the box is a very capable worker that waits for instructions. BrotherME turns it into a careful colleague. Before building anything, it agrees the goal and direction with you. While working, it tells you what is happening in plain language, keeps a written record of decisions and progress that survives crashes and restarts, and brings you bad news first. And when it says something is done, that claim is supposed to come with proof: a check that ran after the last change and passed. On Claude Code that rule is backed by mechanical gates, and those gates fail open rather than lock you out, so it is a strong default, not a guarantee.

You never need to learn the machinery. If you can describe what you want and answer a few plain questions, you can use it.

The philosophy, and the laws

BrotherME is built on one uncomfortable belief: an AI's confidence is not evidence, and a rule written in a prompt is not a control. Anyone can tell a model to be careful; the telling changes little. What makes this product special is that its rules are laws in the working sense: each one is either enforced by machinery, or physically handed to you, and the pages describing them are themselves under test. This is the why behind everything else on this page.

1. Look before touching. Every working session starts by mapping what is already true: what changed, what is mid-flight, who holds what. Work found in progress is coordinated with, never overwritten.
2. One writer per file. A claim on files is written down before work starts, and a mechanical gate refuses a second writer while the claim lives. Parallel work dies by silent overwrites, not by bad code; this is the law that makes parallel safe.
3. Proof, or it is not done. The word done requires a check that ran after the last change and passed. A confident summary is the cheapest thing an AI can produce; a passing check is the only currency accepted here.
4. The guided loop. The strongest mind plans and judges; cheaper hands execute; a helper that fails its check twice is replaced by a stronger one, with the failure attached. Judgment and labor deserve different prices.
5. Ranges, never promises. Every estimate carries its confidence and the assumption most likely to move it, and what actually happened corrects the next forecast. A single number is a lie about certainty.
6. Bad news first, labels on numbers. A failing check leads the message instead of ending it, and a number that could not be verified carries its label right where you read it, never in a footnote.
7. You hold the keys. Decisions arrive one card at a time with a recommendation first. Anything risky (credentials, payments, publishing, deleting) is prepared completely, then handed to you. It never types your passwords, anywhere, ever.
8. Memory lives on disk. Decisions, progress, and mistakes are written to plain files that survive crashes, restarts, and the AI's own forgetting. The record is the truth; recollection is not.
9. Your corrections become law, with your yes. Correct it once and the correction can become a standing rule, retrieved before similar work from then on. Only you can promote one, and no rule can ever weaken the laws above.
10. Wrong claims are corrected in daylight. A sentence that turns out false gets a dated correction on top of it, never a silent rewrite. The sequence is the honest story, and this product keeps its own mistakes visible on principle.

Honest scope, as always: the mechanical enforcement behind laws two and three runs on Claude Code; on other tools these laws travel as written instructions, which is a weaker thing, and the product says so rather than pretending otherwise.

Using the loops well

Under the surface, every piece of delegated work runs through the same loop you just watched: the strongest model plans and judges, cheaper and faster models do the labor. Three things make that work well for you.

Let the cheap helper do the labor, and judge the result by the check, not the prose. A confident paragraph is not proof. A check that ran and passed is.
If a helper fails its check twice, the work moves up a grade on its own, with the failure attached. You do not have to notice, and you do not have to ask.
Your job is decisions. Its job is proof. When taste is needed, it comes to you with one recommendation. When a claim is made, it has to survive a check first.

A small ask and a big build

Ask for one small thing (rename a file, draft one email) and BrotherME takes the shortest path: no ceremony, no paperwork, the change and its check. It sizes up every task before touching anything, and a task that is one step, familiar, and cheap to undo gets treated exactly that lightly. The rules wake up only when the work gets heavy, and that is deliberate: the machinery scales with the ask.

The small ask: minutes

"Rename the product from Swift to Nimbus, everywhere."

What wakes upAlmost nothing. It finds every mention, changes them in one pass, and runs one check: a search proving zero old names survive, run after the last change.
What you seeOne line before, one line after, done. No brief, no forecast, no decision cards. The machinery stays asleep because nothing here needs it.

The big build: three weeks

"A booking product for my tour company: the app, the marketing site, the data behind both, in English, Japanese and Spanish."

What wakes upEverything, in order. A one-page brief you approve. Four lanes of work, each behind its own wall so nothing overwrites anything. Cheap helpers doing the labor, the strongest model judging it, failures escalating on their own. A forecast in ranges that gets corrected by what actually happened. Decision cards when your taste is needed, one at a time.
What you seeOne morning status instead of four scrollbacks. Bad news first when a check fails. At the end, a delivery packet: what was built, every check that ran after the last change, what was left out on purpose and what adding it would take.

The big build is where it earns its keep, because that shape is exactly where AI work usually falls apart: one session forgets what the last one decided, two parallel efforts overwrite each other, confident summaries hide failures, and by week three nobody can say what was actually verified. Each of those failure shapes has a named counter here: written state that survives any crash or restart, one writer per file enforced by a gate, parallel helpers behind separate walls, forecasts that are ranges and get corrected by actuals, and a delivery rule that refuses the word done without a passing check. On a one-hour task you barely notice these. Across a three-week build, they compound into the difference between shipping and starting over. The engineer personas further down show the same machinery carrying real technical weight.

What makes it different

Eight mechanisms. For each: what it does, how you use it, when it matters. This section is kept current by rule: any release that changes a feature updates this section in the same change.

The guided loop

The strongest model plans and judges; cheaper helpers execute; a helper that fails its check twice is replaced by a stronger one.

How to use itAutomatic on any delegated work. Ask for the advanced view if you want to see the routing.
When it mattersAny project big enough to hand pieces away. It is why big builds get top-grade judgment without paying top-grade prices for routine labor.

One writer per file

Before anything writes, its claim on those files is written down, and a mechanical gate refuses a second writer while the claim lives.

How to use itAutomatic. Ask who is working on what to see the live claims.
When it mattersThe moment two things are in flight at once. It is the reason parallel work is safe here and a free-for-all everywhere else.

Proof or it is not done

Done requires a check that ran after the last change and passed; delivery fills a written packet with the evidence.

How to use it/brotherme-review checks work in progress; /brotherme-deliver refuses to close without proof.
When it mattersEvery handoff. It is the standing guard against confident nonsense.

Honest forecasting

Every estimate is a range with a confidence level and the assumption most likely to move it; actuals feed the next forecast.

How to use itIt arrives inside every plan and status. Ask how long at any moment.
When it mattersDeadlines, budgets, and the decision to start at all.

Memory that survives

Decisions, progress, and open questions live on disk, in your project folder and a private records folder, and work is saved right before the AI's memory gets condensed.

How to use itAutomatic. After any crash or restart, /brotherme-status reads the thread back.
When it mattersAny work longer than one sitting. It is why week three still knows what week one decided.

Rules that stick

Correct it once in plain words and the correction can become a stored rule, retrieved before similar work; hard rules act as gates, and turning a correction into a rule always requires your explicit yes.

How to use itSay the correction. It will offer to keep it; you approve or decline.
When it mattersAnything you never want to explain twice, from never publish without my say-so to always show me two options.

Decisions through you, one at a time

Choices arrive as one question with a recommended option first and the tradeoff stated, never a wall of text.

How to use itAutomatic register. Answer the card; work continues.
When it mattersAlways. It is how a non-technical owner keeps real control without reading logs.

Risky moments come back to you

Sign-ins, payments, publishing, and anything destructive stay yours: it prepares the exact step with its undo, then hands you the button. It never types, stores, or logs your credentials.

How to use itAutomatic. When a step needs your accounts or could destroy something, it arrives prepared, with its rollback, and waits for you.
When it mattersProduction systems, money, anything public. It is why you can hand over the labor without handing over the keys.

What it is good at, and what it is not

Good at

  • Turning a plain-words idea into a checked, delivered result
  • Scoped building: websites, reports, data cleanups, prototypes
  • Catching its own failures: separate checkers try to break the work
  • Surviving crashes: progress and decisions live on disk, not in memory
  • Saying no: it is built to refuse calling unproven work done, with that refusal enforced on Claude Code only

Not good at

  • Pure taste calls: it recommends, but your eye decides
  • Work with no possible check: if nothing can prove it, it says so
  • Anything needing your accounts: payments, sign-ins, publishing credentials stay with you, and it hands those moments back
  • Real work of any length: every result so far comes from test suites and simulated projects, not from a day of anyone's actual work. You may be the first.
  • Unattended runs: nobody has left it working alone for long, and it is not claimed to survive that
  • Being fast on tiny tasks: the checking adds minutes, for a one-line answer, ask plain Claude

Who it is built for, seven people deep

Seven personas, from no technical background to plenty of it. Told with the same honesty as everything else on this page: these are designed-for stories, and none of these people exists as a customer yet.

The first-time founder

No technical background, runs on plain words and trust that has to be earned.

Their deep caseThe bakery story above is their story shape. They type /brotherme-start, describe the outcome, answer three questions, approve a one-page brief, then decide only when a decision card arrives, one at a time with a recommendation first. They never meet a technical term unless they ask. When they come back after a week away, /brotherme-status reads the whole thread back in eight plain lines.
What carries themProof or it is not done, decisions through you, honest forecasting, memory that survives.

The solo founder with several irons in the fire

Seasoned, impatient, delegating hard, running more than one project at once.

Their deep caseA product rebuild in four lanes at once: the app, the site, the data work, the launch copy. Each lane runs behind its own wall, so four parallel efforts cannot overwrite each other; the guided loop sends bulk labor to cheap helpers while judgment stays on the strongest model; every morning starts with one /brotherme-status view instead of four scrollbacks. When they correct course once (never announce a launch before legal reads the copy), that correction is offered as a stored rule and resurfaces on every future launch as a gate. When a contractor joins in week two, the deep tour behind /brotherme-help generates the where-do-we-stand page that replaces a day of onboarding calls.
What carries themThe guided loop, one writer per file, rules that stick, the deep tour.

The seasoned builder

A developer or technical individual contributor who wants to write code alongside it, not watch from the passenger seat.

Their deep caseThey join mid-project and take the deep tour: the live code map, the data model, every decision with its reasoning. Then they claim the module they want to write by hand, and the same gate that keeps two helpers from colliding now keeps helpers out of THEIR files while they work in their own editor. Their code lands under the same law as everyone else's: tests beside the code, a check after the last change, review by a reader who did not write it. When they want to extend BrotherME itself, the co-build section shows the house conventions: mirror the closest sibling file, give every risky call an explicit failure path, keep the seam small.
What carries themOne writer per file shared between humans and helpers, proof or it is not done, the deep tour.

The data engineer

Owns the pipelines and the warehouse. Lives and dies by numbers being right.

Their deep caseRebuilding the orders mart that six dashboards sit on, fed by three source systems that disagree. The brief pins what correct means before any SQL is written: row counts reconcile against each source, revenue matches finance's monthly total, and the six dashboards keep working. Helpers draft the bulk transformations, and the house rule for all data work is that every headline number gets re-derived a second, independent way before anyone sees it, so the mart's totals are checked by a query that does not share the mart's own joins. A separate checker hunts fan-out: it plants duplicate keys on purpose and fails the build if a join multiplies them. The backfill script and the model rewrite run in parallel without colliding, because each file has one writer at a time. And in week three, the week-one decision about late-arriving orders is still on disk, quoted in the delivery packet next to the reconciliation runs that prove the cutover.
What carries themProof or it is not done, second-way checks on every number, one writer per file, memory that survives.

The backend engineer

Ships APIs other teams build against. A wrong error path at 2 a.m. is their phone ringing.

Their deep caseSplitting one overloaded payments endpoint into a clean, versioned interface. The plan is contract first: the new interface and everyone who consumes it are written down and agreed before implementation starts. The schema migration is rehearsed in both directions against a restored copy, and the rollout plan carries a rollback beside every step, written before the step runs. Helpers draft handlers and tests, but the house law is that every call across a boundary (network, database, queue) shows its explicit failure path, and a separate checker attacks exactly those paths: timeouts, double-sends, malformed payloads, a queue that answers late. Done is refused until the suite runs green after the last edit. And the moment anything needs production access, the prepared command comes back with its undo and waits, because it holds no keys to production and never will.
What carries themRisky moments come back to you, checkers built to break error paths, proof or it is not done.

The infrastructure engineer

Owns the clusters, the infrastructure code, and the pipeline everyone else's merges run through.

Their deep caseA cluster upgrade that has to happen across several evenings. BrotherME drafts the runbook: every command in order, each with its rollback beside it and its dry-run output attached, reviewed before anything real runs. It holds no apply rights on production, as a law rather than a setting: plans and diffs are its work, the apply is the engineer's own hand, every time. Module work runs in parallel safely because each module has one writer at a time, and free disk is checked before heavy builds instead of failing halfway through one. When evening two starts, the runbook's exact position is read back from disk rather than reconstructed from memory. And when the upgrade is finally declared done, that claim carries the verification commands that ran after the last change, not a paragraph of confidence.
What carries themRisky moments come back to you, one writer per file, memory that survives, honest status.

The engineering lead

Runs a small team plus AI helpers, and owns what quality means for both.

Their deep caseFour workstreams at once: a feature, a refactor, a data fix, the release notes. Each runs behind its own wall, so the team's humans and the AI helpers stop overwriting each other, and the walls double as review boundaries, because who owns which files is written down instead of remembered. When the lead corrects course once, in plain words (no schema change ships without its migration plan), that correction is offered back as a standing rule, takes effect only with an explicit yes, and then resurfaces on every future schema task. A new joiner takes the deep tour behind /brotherme-help and gets the live map (state, decisions, code layout) instead of two days of onboarding calls. And the session records show, week by week, where forecasts drifted from actuals, so next month's plan argues from evidence.
What carries themRules that stick, one writer per file, the deep tour, honest forecasting.

Install it, and keep it updated

Open Claude Code and type these two commands, one at a time:

/plugin marketplace add khalilmaaouni/BrotherModeUp
/plugin install brotherme

If Claude Code asks which source to install from, pick the one you just added, and restart if it tells you to. That is the whole install. To check it worked, type /brotherme-help. To remove it later, type /plugin uninstall brotherme; your projects and records stay untouched.

To update, the path depends on how you installed it. If you used the plugin, type these two lines:

/plugin marketplace update brotherme-marketplace
/plugin update brotherme

Then restart Claude Code. Or skip the two lines entirely: type /brotherme-update. It checks your version against the newest release and walks you through updating, in plain words.

If instead you cloned the repository, update it inside the skill folder:

git fetch --tags
git checkout <newest tag>

The seven commands

/brotherme-startBegin a project. It asks what you want to accomplish and turns your answers into a one-page project brief you approve before anything is built.
/brotherme-statusWhere things stand: goal, progress, time remaining, any decision waiting on you, and the evidence behind each claim.
/brotherme-nextThe single best next step, with a reason and an honest time range.
/brotherme-reviewChecks finished work against a written definition of done, point by point, and never softens a failing point.
/brotherme-deliverWraps up. Delivery requires proof; without a passing check it tells you plainly what remains instead.
/brotherme-helpExplains what you have and what to do next, in plain words.
/brotherme-updateChecks your version against the newest release and walks you through updating, in plain words.

Honest limits, before you rely on it

  • This is release candidate 2.0.0-rc.11, not a finished 2.0.0. No day of real founder work has yet gone through it: everything here rests on test suites and simulated projects. The remaining step is someone using it on live work, and that has not happened yet.
  • The two-line install above has been through its full cycle exactly once, on the author's machine. You may be the first person to run it from GitHub. If it misbehaves, the repository's issue page wants to hear about it.
  • It is verified on Claude Code only. Other AI coding tools get advisory files, not enforced guarantees.
  • Use one install path, not both. The plugin and the older clone-based install wire the same automation; a machine carrying both runs everything twice.
